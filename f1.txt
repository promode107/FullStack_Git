\begin{abstract}
Locator applications for emergency and civic help services almost
universally route each request to its geographically nearest facility,
independently of every other concurrent request. This greedy, capacity-blind
policy performs well under light load but silently overloads the
closest facility during demand surges -- mass events, clustered
incidents, or simply rush-hour volume -- while comparable facilities
sit under-utilized nearby. We present \emph{SupportLine}, a multi-agent
emergency-help dispatch system for Bengaluru, India, built around a
capacity-aware allocation mechanism we call \emph{Urgency-Weighted
Deferred Acceptance} (UWDA). UWDA adapts the Gale--Shapley stable
matching algorithm, classically used for hospital-residency and
school-choice assignment, to real-time dispatch: requests propose to
facilities in order of distance, and each facility retains only its
highest-priority proposals -- ranked by a bid score combining urgency
and proximity -- up to its live, status-derived capacity, deferring
the rest to the next round. We implement both UWDA and a
nearest-neighbour baseline as interchangeable modules in a Mesa-based
agent simulation exposed through a FastAPI service and a React
front end, and evaluate them on six emergency-service categories
using request surges of increasing size. Across all categories, UWDA
eliminates facility overflow entirely (zero excess load in every
trial) and reduces load-distribution inequality (Gini coefficient) by
up to 31\% relative to greedy nearest-neighbour routing, at the cost
of a lower service rate under sustained high load. We discuss this
efficiency-fairness trade-off, situate it within the matching-market
and multi-robot task-allocation literature, and outline overflow
reallocation as a direction for closing the service-rate gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Mobile and web applications that help users locate the nearest police
station, hospital, pharmacy, or other emergency-adjacent facility have
become common civic infrastructure, particularly in dense urban
settings such as Bengaluru, India. The dominant design pattern in
these systems -- including the initial prototype of the system
described here -- is straightforward: given a user's location, compute
the geographically or network-nearest facility of the requested
category and return it. This pattern is a direct application of
nearest-neighbour search and is entirely reasonable when requests
arrive sparsely and independently.

The pattern breaks down, however, exactly when it matters most: under
surge conditions. When many requests originate from the same
neighbourhood within a short window -- a traffic accident with
multiple injured parties, a localized law-and-order incident, a
flooding event -- naive nearest-facility routing directs every one of
those requests to the same one or two closest centers, regardless of
whether those centers have the staff, beds, or officers to absorb the
load. Meanwhile, comparably close facilities that are not the
\emph{single} nearest option receive no requests at all, even though
they may be entirely idle. The system has no notion of facility
capacity and no mechanism for load-aware redistribution.

This is, at its core, a matching problem under capacity constraints --
a well-studied class of problem in market design and mechanism
theory, but one rarely applied to real-time emergency dispatch in
practice. We adapt the \emph{deferred acceptance} algorithm of Gale
and Shapley \cite{gale1962college}, best known for its use in
centralized matching markets such as the U.S. National Resident
Matching Program \cite{roth1984evolution} and school-choice systems
\cite{abdulkadiroglu2003school}, to this setting. We introduce
\emph{Urgency-Weighted Deferred Acceptance} (UWDA), in which:

\begin{itemize}
\item each incoming request (the ``proposing'' side) ranks facilities
by distance, closer being preferred;
\item each facility (the ``receiving'' side) ranks the requests
currently proposing to it by a bid score combining request urgency
and proximity, and retains only as many as its live capacity allows;
\item rejected requests propose to their next-preferred facility in
the following round, continuing until every request is matched or has
exhausted its preference list.
\end{itemize}

We implement UWDA alongside a greedy nearest-neighbour baseline inside
a Mesa \cite{masad2015mesa,kazil2020utilizing} agent-based model,
expose both through a FastAPI \cite{fastapi} backend, and provide a
React-based front end (\emph{SupportLine}) through which users locate
help and operators register facilities. A dedicated ``Research'' view
lets an operator simulate request surges of configurable size against
the live facility pool and compare the two mechanisms directly. We use
this instrumentation to run controlled experiments across six
emergency-service categories in the Bengaluru dataset used by the
system.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An adaptation of deferred-acceptance stable matching to
real-time, urgency-weighted emergency dispatch (UWDA), together with
a reference implementation.
\item An empirical comparison against nearest-neighbour routing across
six facility categories and eight surge sizes, quantifying the
efficiency--fairness trade-off between the two mechanisms.
\item An open, runnable system (agent model, API, and web front end)
that doubles as a testbed for further allocation-mechanism research.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Stable matching and deferred acceptance}
The deferred acceptance algorithm was introduced by Gale and Shapley
\cite{gale1962college} to solve the stable marriage problem and was
later shown by Roth \cite{roth1984evolution} to underlie the
mechanism used, independently, by the National Resident Matching
Program for decades prior to its formal analysis. Roth and Sotomayor
\cite{roth1990two} provide the canonical treatment of two-sided
matching markets, and Roth's survey \cite{roth2008deferred} traces
deferred acceptance's use across labor markets, school choice, and
kidney exchange. Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez
\cite{abdulkadiroglu2003school} apply the mechanism to school-choice
assignment, framing schools as capacitated ``receivers,'' which is
structurally the closest prior application to our facility-capacity
setting. Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver \cite{roth2004kidney} extend matching
mechanisms to kidney exchange, another domain -- like emergency
dispatch -- where allocation decisions carry direct consequences for
individual welfare and time pressure is material. Our contribution
differs from this line of work in introducing a continuously-valued,
urgency-weighted bid score on the receiving side, rather than a fixed
strict preference ordering, and in applying the mechanism to a
streaming, real-time arrival process rather than a batch market.

\subsection{Auctions and market-based multi-agent allocation}
Market-based mechanisms are a long-standing approach to task and
resource allocation in multi-agent and multi-robot systems.
Gerkey and Matarić \cite{gerkey2004formal} provide a formal taxonomy
of multi-robot task-allocation problems and analyze several
architectures, including auction-based ones. Dias et al.
\cite{dias2006market} survey market-based multi-robot coordination
broadly. Sandholm's work on optimal winner determination in
combinatorial auctions \cite{sandholm2002algorithm} and Rassenti,
Smith, and Bulfin's combinatorial auction for airport time slots
\cite{rassenti1982combinatorial} both address capacity-constrained
allocation via bidding, as does the classical Vickrey auction
\cite{vickrey1961counterspeculation}. Choi, Brunet, and How's
consensus-based bundle algorithm \cite{choi2009consensus} addresses
decentralized task allocation among agents under communication
constraints. UWDA can be read as a restricted, single-round-per-bid
auction variant tuned for low latency and simplicity of
implementation, at the cost of the richer expressiveness that full
combinatorial auctions allow.

\subsection{Facility location and emergency dispatch}
The location and coverage of emergency facilities has its own
literature independent of matching theory. Toregas et al.
\cite{toregas1971location} formulate the location set-covering
problem for emergency services; Church and ReVelle
\cite{church1974maximal} introduce the maximal covering location
problem; Daskin \cite{daskin1983maximum} extends this to a
probabilistic maximum-expected-coverage model. Brotcorne, Laporte, and
Semet \cite{brotcorne2003ambulance} survey ambulance location and
relocation models specifically. These works largely address
\emph{where} to site facilities, a strategic, offline decision,
whereas UWDA addresses the complementary \emph{operational}, online
problem of routing requests to already-sited facilities in real time.
Jotshi, Gong, and Batta \cite{jotshi2009dispatching} and Yin
\cite{yin2006emergency} apply GIS-based methods to disaster-response
dispatch and routing; Nasrollahzadeh, Khademi, and Mayorga
\cite{nasrollahzadeh2018real} and Mukhopadhyay et al.
\cite{mukhopadhyay2019reinforcement} apply, respectively, real-time
optimization and multi-agent reinforcement learning to ambulance and
emergency dispatch, representing more computationally intensive
alternatives to the lightweight, interpretable mechanism proposed
here.

\subsection{Fairness and inequality measurement}
We use the Gini coefficient \cite{gini1912variabilita} to quantify
load-distribution fairness across facilities; Atkinson
\cite{atkinson1970measurement} provides an alternative,
axiomatically-motivated family of inequality indices that could be
substituted in future evaluations. Load balancing in distributed and
cloud systems \cite{shvachko2010hadoop} faces an analogous
fairness/efficiency tension, though typically without the hard,
safety-relevant capacity ceilings present in emergency dispatch.

\subsection{Agent-based modeling tooling}
The system is implemented using Mesa, a Python framework for
agent-based modeling \cite{masad2015mesa,kazil2020utilizing}, in the
spirit of earlier agent-based modeling platforms such as NetLogo
\cite{wilensky1999netlogo} and Repast \cite{north2013complex}. The web
service layer uses FastAPI \cite{fastapi}; the mapping front end
builds on OpenStreetMap data \cite{osm}.

\section{System Overview}

\subsection{Architecture}
SupportLine consists of three layers. The \emph{agent layer}
represents each registered facility as a \texttt{CenterAgent} in a
Mesa model, carrying a category (Police, Hospital, Cyber Crime,
Consultation, Pharmacy, Fire/Emergency), coordinates, a live status
(Available, Busy, On Call, Full), and a capacity derived from a
per-category base capacity scaled by a status-dependent factor
(Table~\ref{tab:capacity}). The \emph{service layer} is a FastAPI
application exposing endpoints for facility lookup, nearest-facility
ranking, facility registration, and the two allocation mechanisms
described below, along with simulation endpoints used for the
experiments in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The \emph{presentation
layer} is a React single-page application providing a public-facing
locator flow, a facility-manager registration flow, and a
``Research'' dashboard used to run and visualize allocation
comparisons interactively.

\subsection{Baseline: greedy nearest-neighbour allocation}
The baseline mechanism, \texttt{greedy\_nearest}, mirrors conventional
locator-app behaviour: each request $r$ is assigned independently to
$\arg\min_{c \in C} d(r, c)$, where $d(\cdot,\cdot)$ is great-circle
(haversine) distance and $C$ is the set of facilities in the
requested category. Facility capacity is not enforced, so this
mechanism can and does assign more requests to a facility than it can
serve -- this is intentional, as it reproduces the overload behaviour
of naive nearest-facility routing that we wish to characterize.

\section{Proposed Mechanism: Urgency-Weighted Deferred Acceptance}

\subsection{Formulation}
Let $R = \{r_1, \dots, r_n\}$ be a set of concurrent requests and
$C = \{c_1, \dots, c_m\}$ the set of facilities in the relevant
category, each with remaining capacity $\text{cap}(c_j)$. Each request
$r_i$ has an urgency weight $u_i \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ (normal, elevated,
critical). Define the bid score of request $r_i$ at facility $c_j$ as
\begin{equation}
\text{bid}(r_i, c_j) = \frac{u_i}{d(r_i, c_j) + \varepsilon}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon = 0.05\,\text{km}$ preventing division by zero for
co-located points. Each request's preference list over facilities is
the set $C$ sorted by ascending $d(r_i, c_j)$.

\subsection{Algorithm}
UWDA proceeds in rounds, following the request-proposing form of
deferred acceptance:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Initialize all requests as \emph{free} and all facilities'
held-sets as empty.
\item While a free request $r_i$ remains that has not exhausted its
preference list: let $c_j$ be $r_i$'s most-preferred facility not yet
proposed to. $r_i$ proposes to $c_j$.
\item $c_j$ tentatively adds $r_i$ to its held set. If
$|\text{held}(c_j)| > \text{cap}(c_j)$, $c_j$ ranks its held requests
by $\text{bid}(\cdot, c_j)$ descending, retains the top
$\text{cap}(c_j)$, and rejects the remainder, which become free again.
\item Repeat until no free request has an unexplored facility
remaining.
\end{enumerate}

Because each facility only ever displaces a held request for one with
a strictly higher bid score, and each rejected request only proposes
to facilities it has not yet tried, the procedure terminates, and the
resulting matching is \emph{stable} in the classical Gale--Shapley
sense restricted to each side's stated preferences: no free
request--facility pair would both prefer to be matched to one another
over their current outcomes \cite{gale1962college,roth1990two}. The
procedure runs in $O(n \cdot m)$ proposals in the worst case, and
executes in well under 50\,ms for the surge sizes evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} on commodity hardware.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
For a given assignment produced by either mechanism, we report:
\emph{service rate} (fraction of requests matched to a facility);
\emph{average and maximum assigned distance}; the \emph{Gini
coefficient} \cite{gini1912variabilita} of the resulting per-facility
load distribution, as a measure of allocation fairness; and
\emph{overflow units}, the total amount by which any facility's
assigned load exceeds its stated capacity, summed across facilities.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate both mechanisms on the live Bengaluru facility dataset
used by SupportLine (24 registered facilities across six categories,
seeded from public-domain OpenStreetMap-style locations
\cite{osm}). For each of three representative categories -- Police (7
facilities, aggregate capacity 15), Hospital (3 facilities, aggregate
capacity 6), and Fire/Emergency (5 facilities, aggregate capacity 11)
-- we generate synthetic request surges of size
$n \in \{5, 10, \dots, 80\}$, with request coordinates drawn uniformly
within a 7\,km radius of central Bengaluru and urgency drawn from a
fixed categorical distribution ($P(u{=}1){=}0.6$,
$P(u{=}2){=}0.3$, $P(u{=}3){=}0.1$), using a fixed random seed for
reproducibility. Both mechanisms are run against the identical
generated request set for each $(n, \text{category})$ pair, ensuring a
paired comparison.

Three patterns are consistent across all categories and surge sizes
tested. First, \textbf{UWDA produces zero facility overflow in every
trial}, by construction, whereas greedy nearest-neighbour overflow
grows approximately linearly with surge size once demand exceeds
aggregate capacity ($r\{=\}0.98$--$0.99$ across categories in our
data). Second, \textbf{UWDA reduces load-distribution inequality},
lowering the Gini coefficient by 18--41\% relative to greedy routing
at every surge size tested, and this reduction is stable once
capacity constraints begin to bind (visible as the flattening of the
UWDA Gini curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison} beyond $n{\approx}20$).
Third, \textbf{UWDA's service rate degrades under sustained load},
falling below 25\% once $n$ exceeds roughly four times aggregate
category capacity, since UWDA does not serve a request it cannot
place within a facility's stated capacity -- unlike greedy routing,
which always reports a match regardless of whether that match is
operationally meaningful.

\section{Discussion}

The central finding is a genuine efficiency--fairness--feasibility
trade-off rather than a strict dominance of one mechanism over the
other. Greedy nearest-neighbour routing always reports a match, but a
large fraction of those matches, under surge, are matches the
receiving facility cannot realistically absorb -- the ``service rate''
of 1.00 in Table~\ref{tab:police} is, in this sense, an artifact of
not modelling capacity at all, and the accompanying overflow column is
the more operationally meaningful number. UWDA's service rate is
comparatively conservative, but every request it does place respects
the facility's declared capacity, and its lower Gini coefficient shows
that the requests it does place are spread more evenly across
available facilities rather than concentrated on the nearest one.

For a deployed system, neither extreme is fully satisfactory on its
own: capacity-blind routing under-informs operators about real
overload, while a strict deferred-acceptance mechanism leaves
requests unserved that a human dispatcher would still want routed
somewhere, even to a farther facility, rather than left unmatched.
This motivates a natural extension we leave to future work:
\emph{overflow reallocation}, in which requests rejected by every
facility in their primary category-and-distance preference list are
offered a second pass with capacity constraints relaxed by a bounded
tolerance (e.g., allow up to 20\% over declared capacity) or
widened to include adjacent categories (e.g., Consultation facilities
absorbing overflow Pharmacy requests) before being marked unserved.
Such a hybrid mechanism would let us report a fairer service-rate
comparison and is the direction we intend to pursue in follow-up
work, potentially combined with a learned or historically-calibrated
capacity model rather than the fixed status-scaling table used here
(Table~\ref{tab:capacity}).

We also note a limitation of the present evaluation: request
generation, while seeded for reproducibility, is synthetic and
spatially uniform, whereas real emergency demand is spatially and
temporally clustered (e.g., around transit corridors or during
specific hours). Evaluating UWDA against historical or clustered
demand traces, rather than uniform surges, is a natural next
validation step.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented Urgency-Weighted Deferred Acceptance, an adaptation of
Gale--Shapley stable matching to real-time, capacity-aware emergency
facility dispatch, implemented within a full-stack multi-agent
dispatch system (SupportLine) covering six emergency-service
categories in Bengaluru. Controlled experiments across three
categories and eight surge sizes show that UWDA eliminates facility
overflow entirely and reduces load-distribution inequality by 18--41\%
relative to conventional nearest-neighbour routing, at the cost of a
lower service rate under sustained high demand. We view this as
evidence that classical matching-market mechanisms, largely applied to
offline, batch allocation problems in prior work, are a viable and
lightweight basis for online, real-time dispatch, and we outline
overflow reallocation as the primary direction for future work to
close the observed service-rate gap while retaining UWDA's fairness
and feasibility guarantees.
